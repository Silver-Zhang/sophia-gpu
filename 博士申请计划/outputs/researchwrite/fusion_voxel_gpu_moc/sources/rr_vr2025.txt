Abstract
Deep penetration radiation shielding problems often use Monte Carlo augmented
with weight windows to obtain sufficiently low variances in acceptable run times.
In this paper, the random ray method was proposed as an approach to generating
weight windows. Compared to deterministic alternatives, it allows for straight-
forward spatial discretisation and continuously samples angular phase space. The
methods implemented were tested on two shielding problems, examining global vari-
ance reduction and source-detector variance reduction. In both cases, the figure of
merit of the random ray based methods is at least 50 times higher than analog
Monte Carlo, and in the Global Variance Reduction model it is comparable to that
of MAGIC, a popular fully Monte Carlo approach to weight window generation.
The methods implemented are also robust to modelling choices like the source of
multi group cross sections (NJOY or Monte Carlo), scattering anisotropy and the
order of the random ray spatial source representation.
Keywords — Variance Reduction, The Random Ray Method, FW-CADIS, CADIS

I. INTRODUCTION
Some applications of interest for nuclear engineering, such as fusion reactor
design and radiation shielding problems, are generally simulated with Monte Carlo
(MC) methods and require variance reduction (VR) to produce acceptable results.
VR techniques normally work by producing and reading importance maps or weight
windows to direct particles towards the locations of interest. One method to em-
ploy weight window maps is in combination with particle splitting and rouletting:
splitting particles when their weight is higher than the local upper weight window
bound, and applying Russian roulette when their weight is less than the lower weight
window bound. Alternatively, weight windows can inform techniques like exponen-
tial transforms [1] or adaptive multilevel splitting [2]. However, the generation of
appropriate weight windows is nontrivial, and there are several possible ways to do
it.
The most common way to produce weight window maps is Consistent Adjoint-
Driven Importance Sampling (CADIS), which relates the weight boundaries in each
phase space region to a particle’s local importance, i.e, the adjoint flux [3]. The
adjoint flux is normally obtained with a deterministic solver. For example, MCNP
[4] is coupled with the ad hoc software ADVANTG [5], which produces importance
maps by running the discrete ordinates (S N) code Denovo [6]. Both TRIPOLI [7]
and MCBEND [8] have an in-built adjoint diffusion solver. Clearly, all these codes
need to have multi group cross sections available for the problem of interest before
running their deterministic solvers.
Serpent [9], on the other hand, uses an internal adjoint response matrix solver
to generate importance maps [10] which is CADIS-inspired but employs different
definitions. The importance in this case is related to the adjoint current entering
each cell of the importance map. This solver does not require multi group cross
sections, but coupling coefficients between different mesh cells, tallied by Monte
Carlo. Since the response matrix mesh must cover all the regions of interest, and
particles might not reach each cell of the mesh during an analog Monte Carlo cal-
culation, as is required for tallying the coupling coefficients, this approach requires
an iterative solution. Each iteration, the weight windows calculated by the response
matrix solver are used in a Monte Carlo run to calculate the new coefficients, until
the particle population covers the whole geometry and the final importance map
3
--- PAGE 4 ---
can be produced.
When the goal of a variance reduction calculation is to minimise the variance
at a specific location, the adjoint source for the deterministic calculation is simply
the detector cross section at the detector’s position in phase space. If the calculation
aims at obtaining a uniform statistical uncertainty throughout the problem geometry
(Global Variance Reduction or GVR), or a low uncertainty at multiple detectors,
Forward-Weighted CADIS (FW-CADIS) [11] is used. FW-CADIS requires two steps
to produce weight windows: first, a forward transport calculation is run to obtain
the forward flux, used to generate an adjoint source; then, an adjoint calculation
generates an adjoint flux solution, used to construct importance maps.
In the case of GVR, alternative methods not based on an adjoint flux solution
were also proposed in the past. For example, in [12], it is shown that if the goal
is to obtain a uniform density of simulated particles throughout the model phase
space, the weight distribution w must simply be proportional to the physical neutron
density. One criticism to this approach is that the distribution of the population
might not reflect the distribution of the importance, i.e., the contributions to the
desired tally. However, this approach was shown to produce good results in [12]
and [13], where the forward flux was used as a proxy for the neutron density. The
forward flux solution can be generated in multiple ways: [12] uses a quasi-diffusion
solution, where the necessary diffusion Eddington factors are estimated by Monte
Carlo, whereas [13] uses a pure Monte Carlo route. In both cases, the process is
iterative. In the latter method, called MAGIC in the literature, multi group Monte
Carlo could be used to speed up the iterations prior to a final, continuous energy
iteration. MAGIC is implemented, for example, in OpenMC [14, 15] and SCONE
[16].
In this work, the application of the random ray (RR) method to generate weight
windows is tested with SCONE [16]. CADIS was implemented and tested on a
detector problem, and both the route based on the forward flux and the FW-CADIS
approach are adopted for GVR. The RR method, introduced in [17], is a stochastic
version of the Method of Characteristics (MoC): it uses randomly sampled rays
which vary between iterations rather than a fixed cyclic quadrature as is common
in MoC. This implies that an initial angular flux per each ray cannot be stored;
instead, each ray starts with an initial angular flux guess, but its contributions to
the flux results are not tallied until it travels a fixed ‘dead length’. After the dead
4
--- PAGE 5 ---
length, it is assumed that the error due to the original guess is sufficiently attenuated
to accurately represent the local angular flux. Then, each ray is transported for a
fixed ‘active length’, during which contributions to the scalar flux are recorded. The
sum of the dead length and active length is also referred to as the ‘termination
length’. The use of a stochastic quadrature brings several benefits: the method
is low memory, since angular fluxes at the boundaries are not stored; the model
geometries are easily extendable to 3D, since there is no need for a closed quadrature.
Additionally, the RR method does not discretise the angular variable, so it does not
suffer from ray effects [18].
Although the use of randomly sampled rays introduces statistical uncertain-
ties in the results, the error is distributed relatively uniformly in space and energy
unlike in Monte Carlo [19]. This makes it ideal for supporting Monte Carlo VR
calculations, and of great interest for applications such as radiation shielding and
fusion neutronics. The RR method was only recently implemented in a fixed source
calculation mode, and it proved to be computationally and memory efficient [20].
One of the drawbacks of common hybrid deterministic-Monte Carlo VR ap-
proaches is that a new geometry and input file have to be written to convert the
Monte Carlo model to a deterministic model, unless the two codes are well inte-
grated. However, due to the geometric flexibility allowed by on-the-fly ray tracing,
RR can also directly use CSG Monte Carlo geometries, simplifying the VR process
by removing (or lessening) the burden of constructing two simulation domains and
mapping between them. The most significant difference between geometries is that
RR requires a relatively fine geometric discretisation while Monte Carlo needs none,
but this discretisation can be straightforwardly imposed by introducing a Carte-
sian grid onto the Monte Carlo geometry [21]. Additionally, model-dependent multi
group cross sections must be generated for the RR geometry.
The rest of this paper will describe and evaluate the use of RR for Monte Carlo
VR in more detail and provide numerical results for a detector problem and a deep
penetration shielding problem. In the case of GVR, the two routes employing RR
will be compared with MAGIC, also implemented in SCONE.

II. METHODOLOGY
In this section, the variance reduction methods tested and compared are de-
scribed, together with the details of their implementation in SCONE.
II.A. CADIS
The CADIS methodology was introduced in [3], aiming to obtain zero variance
results at a detector tally in a Monte Carlo calculation. CADIS prescribes which
statistical weight Monte Carlo particles should have in order to obtain zero variance
results at the detector of interest, as a function of the adjoint flux ψ†(r, E, Ω), where
r is a point in space, E is neutron energy, and Ω is the neutron flight direction. For
this purpose, the adjoint source q†(r, E) is equivalent to the cross section at the
detector of interest, Σ d(r, E). The optimal particle weight distribution, w(r, E, Ω),
is then the inverse of the adjoint flux normalised by the response function of interest,

Monte Carlo. Furthermore, an additional approximation is often introduced where
the adjoint angular flux is replaced with the adjoint scalar flux, ϕ†(r, E). This is

Additionally the adjoint flux, and hence the weights, are not known as a contin-
uous distribution, but they are discretised over space and energy and used in Monte
Carlo as a weight window mesh. Because of these approximations, zero variance
results are not realistically achieved. However, the CADIS method greatly helps
in reducing the variance of Monte Carlo results at a detector compared to analog
calculations; the reduction of the variance depends on parameters like the phase
space mesh used for the weight windows, chosen ad-hoc for each model, and the
quality of the deterministic adjoint calculation. The production of weight windows
from CADIS-produced optimal weights is also not specified by the method – either
the user or a code must decide on the ‘width’ of the weight windows. Similarly, the
Russian Roulette survival weight is a free parameter, although it is often set as the
local optimal weight. Finally, the settings of the Monte Carlo code can significantly
affect efficiency, for example, the maximum number of particle splits allowed per
particle or per family of particles.

II.C. Forward flux-based GVR
In the case of GVR, the weight window maps can be constructed directly from
a forward flux estimate. The logic is the following, as explained by [12]: the distri-
bution of ‘physical’ particles, N(r, E), is equal to the distribution of Monte Carlo
7
--- PAGE 8 ---
particles, M(r, E) multiplied by the statistical weight distribution:
N(r, E) = M(r, E)w(r, E) (5)
In the case of GVR, the goal of the calculation is to obtain a uniform uncertainty
over the whole phase space. If the distribution of Monte Carlo particles is uniform
in phase space (M(r, E) ≃ constant), the number of scores to the tallies will also be
approximately constant. This can be achieved by imposing statistical weights that
are proportional to the physical particle density. However, in neutron transport
calculations it is customary to calculate the scalar flux rather than the particle
density. These are two distinct physical quantities, but in practice the scalar flux is
used:
w(r, E) ∝ N(r, E) ≃ ϕ(r, E) . (6)
As mentioned previously, this logic is applied in MAGIC [13]. MAGIC is an
iterative, fully Monte Carlo method, where the scalar flux results from a Monte
Carlo run are used to produce weight windows for the following run. The target
weights are normalised by the maximum flux value in the system:
w(r, E) = ϕ(r, E)
max(ϕ(r, E)) . (7)
The need for more than two iterations (one to generate weight windows and one
to produce final results) arises when particles are not able to populate the whole
geometry in the first analog calculation. The termination criterion for the MAGIC
iterations is whether scores for the forward flux are obtained in every tally region
in the geometry. Afterwards, a final calculation, using the last weight windows
generated, is performed to produce results. MAGIC is convenient because only one
code has to be used to produce and use the weight windows; however, performing
several Monte Carlo iterations might be computationally expensive, and the use of
the forward rather than adjoint flux implies that the weight windows do not contain
information about particle importance.
In this paper, the forward flux approach to generating weight windows was
used with MAGIC and in combination with RR as well (labelled ‘RR - flux’ in
Section III.A). In this case, a forward RR calculation is performed to produce the
8

forward scalar flux, then applied to Monte Carlo target weights as in Eq. (7). The
main advantage of this method compared to MAGIC is that only one RR run is
required to obtain results everywhere in the phase space, rather than several Monte
Carlo iterations. On the other hand, this entails the practical disadvantages of
discretisation and cross section generation.
II.D. Implementation in SCONE
The methods described so far were implemented in SCONE, the Monte Carlo
code under development at the University of Cambridge [16]. The capability to read
and apply weight windows was introduced in the past by [22]. By default, the weight
windows target weight corresponds to the Russian roulette survival weight; the lower
and upper weight windows are then a factor of 2 respectively lower and higher. A
sensitivity study on the width of the weight windows was not performed, as these
values are the default of MCNP [4] and as such are commonly used. When SCONE is
run with OpenMP parallelism and in combination with weight windows, secondary
particles (produced from splitting) are sent to a particle buffer common between
multiple threads. The common buffer, together with a limit on the maximum num-
ber of splits per particle equal to 1000, is used to improve parallel performance and
mitigate problems with long histories [23].
Implementing MAGIC simply consisted of performing multiple Monte Carlo
fixed source iterations; at the end of an iteration, the scalar fluxes scored in the
user-defined weight window mesh are processed and used as weight windows for
the next iteration. When each tally bin has a non-zero score, the code runs the
last iteration, as done in [13]. It is conceivable that the obtained optimal weight
in a given window might have a large uncertainty. In this work, no threshold on
the maximum uncertainty was introduced, nor were there any other treatments to
mitigate the effects of these uncertainties. Once the particles have populated the
whole phase-space, performing additional MAGIC iterations without increasing the
particle population would only marginally improve the uncertainty of the calculated
weight windows, if at all. While an alternative would be to perform another iter-
ation with an increased particle population, the excessive cost of additional Monte
Carlo calculations that do not contribute to the final results makes this undesirable.
However, in order to allow the user the flexibility to choose how much time to spend
9

performing the MAGIC iterations and the desired precision in the generated weight
windows, the possibility to vary population size between iterations was implemented
in SCONE, as well as the option to run the convergence iterations with multi group
cross sections and switch to continuous energy cross sections for the final iteration.
By default, MAGIC is run with continuous energy cross sections and the particle
population in the iterations prior to final results generation is half the population
of the final run.
Since recently, SCONE includes a RR solver for eigenvalue [19] and fixed source
calculations [20]. For more information about the theory of the random ray method
for fixed source problems, the SCONE implementation and the performance com-
pared to multi-group Monte Carlo, the reader can refer to [20].

II.E. NJOY cross section generation
The multi group cross sections used for the RR calculations (and multi group
MAGIC in one instance, see Sec. III.A.3) were produced with the nuclear data
processing code NJOY [25]. NJOY was chosen as a quick and reliable way to gener-
ate cross sections, under the assumption that Monte Carlo generated cross sections
would not be available under realistic use-cases. The production route used the
NJOY modules RECONR, BROADR, GROUPR, and CCCCR for formatting. The
unresolved resonance reconstruction module was not called, since the unresolved
resonance treatment was not used in the reference Monte Carlo calculations. The
thermal cross section generation module for S(α, β) treatments was also ignored, de-
spite one of the test problems being composed of water, because it complicated the
CCCCR formatting excessively. This is a major approximation, that could impact
the quality of the weight windows in the water problem.
The weighting spectrum adopted was a combination of a fast fission spectrum,
a 1/E intermediate spectrum, and a Maxwellian thermal peak. Multi group cross
sections were generated material-wise, since further spatial discretisation is impos-
sible when the flux energy spectra are not known exactly. The background cross
section of isotope i, σi

III.A. Global Variance Reduction
The application of RR to GVR was tested on a deep penetration shielding
problem. The model geometry and dimensions are shown in Fig. 1. There is an
external 2 MeV neutron source, isotropic and uniformly distributed within a UO 2
square cylinder (in pink in the picture), where the uranium consists of only 238U.
The source is contained inside a concrete cask, placed in a room filled with air. The
room is separated from an adjacent room by a 2 m thick concrete wall. The adjacent
room contains a water cylinder representative of a person (dark blue in the figure).
The isotopic compositions of the materials present in the model are reported in
the Appendix. The whole model geometry spans 6 .4 m × 2.4 m × 3.4 m. Vacuum
boundary conditions are applied on all sides of the problem.
The weight window mesh used is Cartesian, with sides of 20 cm in all directions.
This choice is consistent with values found in the literature [13]. Additionally, a
sensitivity study performed varying the mesh cell sides size to 10 cm did not show
large differences in the FoM, while using 5 cm deteriorated performance slightly.
The energy group structure chosen for the RR calculations was ECCO33, normally
used for fast reactor systems, and the overlaid geometrical discretisation (additional
to that from the Monte Carlo geometry cells) was Cartesian, with sides of 5 cm
in all directions (the total number of cells was ∼424,000). The RR parameters
used in all calculations are: 50000 rays per cycle, 500 active cycles, 700 inactive
cycles, 770 cm dead length, and 1770 cm termination length. These settings are
conservative: the ray density is such that nearly all cells are hit each iteration and
the dead length is larger than the corner-to-corner distance of the problem, ensuring
a vacuum boundary condition hit and removal of error from the initial angular flux
guess. The number of inactive cycles was obtained by trial-and-error, inspecting the

significantly, while the active cycles were chosen to ensure reasonably low statistical

III.A.1. Population scaling
Different weight windows can greatly affect the run time of Monte Carlo simula-
tions: the larger the weight attenuation throughout the geometry, the more splitting
happens and the more particles have to be transported. In order to compare GVR
methods that produced different weight windows, and to prevent excessively long
computational times, the initial Monte Carlo particle populations of different runs
were adjusted to keep the run time approximately constant. This led to using 3
billion particles in the analog calculations, and varying between ∼5 and ∼20 million
particles for the variance reduction calculations.
Theoretically, the FoM introduced in Eq. (9) should be independent of the
particle population used. However, when running numerical tests on the deep pene-
tration problem presented in Fig. 1, this did not seem to be the case and had to be

verified. In order to ensure correct scaling of the FoM, several Monte Carlo instances
with different particle populations were run with FW-CADIS generated weight win-
dows. In order to isolate the Monte Carlo noise from the RR noise, the same weight
windows were used in all cases. For each particle population, 10 different instances
were run with different random number seeds. The results of this scaling test are
shown in Fig. 2, where the error bars correspond to 1 standard deviation calculated
between the 10 instances. Fig. 2 shows that the computational time scales exactly
as expected with the particle population. However, while the calculated scaling of

are present between different instances of the same case. The large fluctuations in
the mean standard deviation are driven by the high variance results on the left of
the concrete shield, i.e., furthest from the source, where a large amount of particle
splitting happens. As an example, the large range of run times and mean standard

in Fig. 5 for the the RR - flux case: the high variance might be a consequence of
particles with different statistical weights scoring in the same tally bin. When, for
example, fast energy particles go through the shield skipping over several weight
windows cells at once, they collide and contribute to the local tally with a weight
which could be several orders of magnitude higher than the local weight window
values. This effect is accentuated the steeper the weight window gradient is. It is
possible that this could be mitigated by adopting energy-dependent weight windows;
however, this was not tested in this work.

flux methods. The use of the adjoint flux captures the fact that the region to the

III.A.4. FW-CADIS variations
All the previous RR runs, including the FW-CADIS ones, were run with trans-
port corrected P0 cross sections, and adopting the flat source (FS) approximation.
However, as a result of recent work on the RR solver in SCONE, a higher or-
der anisotropic scattering treatment was implemented, as well as the use of linear
sources (LS) [26]. The aim of this section is to test whether higher fidelity RR
solutions improve the performance of GVR calculations when using FW-CADIS. A
summary of the results of this study, analysing the use of LS and anisotropy up to
P3, is shown in Table V.
Comparing forward RR P0 scattering solutions with FS and LS against a multi
group Monte Carlo solution from previous calculations, used as a reference, shows
that using LS reduces the difference dramatically (from ∼90% average difference to
∼10% given the spatial discretisation used). However, the resulting weight windows
do not improve the continuous energy Monte Carlo performance, which is almost
identical. The higher relative standard deviation and variance, as well as the higher
standard deviation between independent runs, are due to an outlier among the 20
Monte Carlo runs, which reported an average standard deviation of around 20%.
Otherwise, the main difference is that the time required to generate weight windows
with LS doubles given identical ray settings.
In terms of scattering anisotropy order, a comparison with a reference solu-
tion is impossible since multi group Monte Carlo cannot straightforwardly support

anisotropic scattering beyond P1. However, it was observed that the adjoint flux
profiles correctly converge when increasing the anisotropy order: the average differ-
ence between the P1 and P3 adjoint fluxes is∼23%, while this drops to∼2% between

Carlo performance. All the FoMs without WW generation time are largely within

III.A.5. Monte Carlo generated cross sections
The GVR runs employing RR based on the forward flux and FW-CADIS were
repeated using Monte Carlo generated multi group cross sections instead of the
NJOY ones. Although these are likely impractical to use in production runs, this
allows us to investigate the impact of cross section fidelity on GVR performance.
Monte Carlo cross sections are more accurate since they are generated including the
correct flux spectra; additionally, this allows discretising the geometry into unique
cross section regions for the same materials, which is not straightforwardly possible
with NJOY given limited knowledge of the spatial variation in neutron spectrum a
priori. For these calculations, the concrete shield is divided into 8 regions, and the
source and concrete cask into two, as shown in Fig. 8. The multi group structure
is ECCO33, as before. The SCONE multi group cross section generation route is

detailed in [22].
The expectation is that, while the NJOY cross sections are heavily approxi-
mated, the more representative Monte Carlo cross sections could help capture the
importance distribution with higher accuracy, thus improving the performance of
the weight windows. On the other hand the Monte Carlo cross sections, generated
with a run adopting FW-CADIS weight windows, introduce stochastic noise. For
instance, the uncertainty of the capture cross section in the thermal energy groups
in relatively flat, and it increases from ∼0.05% to ∼5% from the rightmost layer of
the shield to the leftmost. The mean uncertainties of the P0 scattering matrix in

III.A.6. Discussion of GVR results
To summarise, the performance of RR-based methods applied to GVR in the
shielding model described is around 50 times better than analog Monte Carlo, per-
forming similarly to MAGIC. When considering the FoM which takes into account
the time required to produce weight windows, FW-CADIS is generally better. This
is the case because MAGIC is penalised by the multiple costly Monte Carlo itera-
tions needed to converge to a set of weight windows. Attempts to speed up weight
window generation time, such as using multi group cross sections or drastically re-
ducing the particle population used for weight window generation, failed to improve
24
--- PAGE 25 ---
the results. However, adopting MAGIC with a population scaling factor larger than
2 would likely produce better results if the particle population of the final run was
increased, e.g., with the purpose of obtaining lower stochastic errors overall.
Although a comparison with FW-CADIS performed by a fully deterministic
code, rather than RR, was not possible in this paper, the stochastic noise intro-
duced in the weight windows by RR was minimal (as shown in Table II) and did not
harm its performance. A more influential factor was how strong the attenuation of
the flux, and hence the weights, was between the neutron source and the other end
of the geometry. The stronger the attenuation, and the lower the weights, the more
particles with extremely low weights are transported without significantly contribut-
ing to the final results. This factor, other than being model dependent, depends on
which solver is used (RR with forward flux or FW-CADIS), which approximation is
applied (FS vs LS, PN scattering) and which cross sections are being used (NJOY
or MC generated), resulting in an uncontrollable and unpredictable performance.
For instance, in this case, increasing the fidelity of the RR solver did not obviously
improve the GVR performance.
More insightful comparisons were hindered by the large variations present be-
tween different repetitions of the same calculation. This high variability can be due
to the independently generated weight windows being very different to each other
because of stochastic noise, as it happened in the “ce MAGIC ×10” case shown in
Table IV, or by the large amount of local particle splitting present in deep penetra-
tion problems. This behaviour is consistent with the GVR methods review reported
in [27]: the performance of GVR methods on deep penetration problems is highly
model dependent and difficult to assess.

The performance of the CADIS algorithm implemented in the RR solver in
SCONE was tested on a source-detector problem. The model, adopted from [28], is

corner (in yellow in the picture), surrounded by fissile material (in pink). A detector
(in orange) is placed on the other corner of the geometry, whose side is 100 cm long.
In between is room temperature water. Also in this case, the multi group cross
sections needed for CADIS are produced by two separate routes: with NJOY and

Monte Carlo. Once again, the Monte Carlo cross sections are more finely discretised
than the NJOY ones: in the picture, the homogenised regions are highlighted by
the different shades of blue. On the outside, framing the water, are steel (gray) and
air (violet). The isotopic compositions of all the materials are reported in [28].
In this case, the result of interest is the neutron flux at the detector in two
energy groups, thermal and fast, where the separation energy between the two is

is split into the same two energy groups as the detector.
The RR discretisation was Cartesian, with sides of 0 .5 cm, resulting in 400,000
cells. The multi group cross section energy grid is CASMO12. The RR parameters

cycles, 150 cm dead length, and 500 cm termination length.
In comparison to the GVR problem, this was less computationally expensive.
As a consequence, instead of varying the particle population between different cases
to keep the Monte Carlo run time approximately constant, the particle population
was fixed. The population used was 1.5 billion particles for each of the analog runs,
and 1 billion particles for each variance reduction run. Also for this model, each
case was repeated 20 times.

IV. CONCLUSIONS
This paper investigates the application of the random ray method, the stochas-
tic version of the deterministic Method of Characteristics, to Monte Carlo variance
reduction. The random ray method was previously shown to have several char-
acteristics that make it convenient for producing weight windows for Monte Carlo
shielding problems: it produces a solution everywhere in the phase space with ap-
proximately uniform uncertainties, it is low memory and easily applicable to large
3D models, and does not suffer from ray effects. In this work, the CADIS and FW-
CADIS methodologies were implemented in the random ray solver of SCONE, and
tested on a GVR and a source-detector VR problem. Under the assumption that
high fidelity multi group cross sections are not available in industrial applications,
RR was run with computationally cheap NJOY cross sections. Then, the impact of
this approximation on the performance of VR was assessed by comparing results pro-
duced with Monte Carlo generated cross sections. Additionally, the effects of higher
fidelity RR solutions, modelling higher order scattering anisotropy rather than P0
transport corrected cross sections and LS rather than the FS approximation, were
studied.
The GVR application was tested on a challenging deep penetration problem,
including two meters of concrete shield. The RR based methods – FW-CADIS
and weight windows generated from a normalised scalar flux calculated from RR –
performed ∼50 times better than analog Monte Carlo, and comparably to MAGIC.
The source-detector VR application was tested on a 2D problem mostly composed
of water, with a neutron source and a detector in opposite corners of the geometry.
The best CADIS results were ∼175 and ∼20 times better than analog Monte Carlo
respectively in the thermal and fast energy group.
In both applications, improving the fidelity of the multi group cross sections
used and of the RR solution did not improve the performance of VR. A more im-
portant factor was how low the particle weights become during a calculation: there
is likely an optimum between ensuring that particles reach the desired tally loca-
tion, while preventing excessive splitting and thus modelling of extremely low weight
30
--- PAGE 31 ---
particles. While these factors are model dependent and difficult to control, these
results confirm that low fidelity cross sections are appropriate for producing weight
windows. However, detailed comparisons were hindered by the high variability in
performance of independent repetitions of the same runs.
Notably, the problems considered presently are ‘bulk shielding’ problems, i.e.,
there are no prominent streaming paths. Conversely, many shielding problems are
dominated by particle streaming through small, low density regions. This is the case
in ‘maze’ geometries, or in portions of fusion systems. In such streaming problems,
obtaining accurate deterministic estimates of the flux (and so the optimal weights
for variance reduction) is challenging. This is due to strong variations in the an-
gular flux with direction and the resulting need for a refined angular quadrature.
In this domain, one would expect the random ray method to be competitive with
alternative deterministic solution methods due to the unique lack of angular dis-
cretisation. Methods such as MAGIC may also struggle in these problems as there
is a much narrower range of directions (compared to the problems considered here)
in which a neutron might travel that would result in contributing to a detector
of interest. Future work may consider applying the random ray method to such
streaming problems in anticipation of greater performance improvements.
